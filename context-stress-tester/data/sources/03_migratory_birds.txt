Every year, billions of birds undertake extraordinary journeys across continents and oceans, traveling between breeding grounds and wintering sites in a phenomenon known as migration. Some species cover truly astonishing distances; the Arctic tern, for example, migrates from its breeding grounds near the Arctic Circle all the way to Antarctica and back again each year, a round trip that can exceed seventy thousand kilometers and is the longest migration of any animal on Earth.

Scientists believe migratory behavior evolved primarily as a response to seasonal changes in food availability and climate. By moving toward regions with more favorable conditions at different times of year, migratory birds can access abundant insect populations, longer daylight hours for feeding chicks, and milder temperatures, even though the journey itself is often extremely dangerous and energy-intensive.

Birds navigate during these long journeys using a remarkable combination of senses and cues. Many species appear to use the position of the sun and stars as compass references, while others seem able to detect the Earth's magnetic field directly, a sense that researchers still do not fully understand. Landmarks such as coastlines, mountain ranges, and river valleys also appear to play a role in helping birds stay on course, particularly for younger individuals making the journey for the first time.

Migration is also an enormously risky undertaking. Birds face threats ranging from exhaustion and starvation to severe weather, predation, and increasingly, human-made hazards such as collisions with buildings, communication towers, and wind turbines. Habitat loss at stopover sites, where birds rest and refuel partway through their journeys, has become a particular concern for conservationists, since the loss of even a single critical stopover location can disrupt migration routes that birds have followed for generations. Protecting these interconnected networks of breeding grounds, migration corridors, and wintering sites requires international cooperation, since a single migratory species may cross dozens of national borders in the course of a single year.
