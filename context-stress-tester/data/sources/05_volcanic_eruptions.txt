Volcanic eruptions occur when molten rock, ash, and gases trapped beneath the Earth's crust find a path to the surface, often with dramatic and sometimes catastrophic results. The behavior of a given eruption depends heavily on the composition of the magma involved. Magma that is relatively low in silica tends to be thin and fluid, allowing gases to escape easily and typically producing gentler, effusive eruptions characterized by flowing lava rather than violent explosions. Magma that is rich in silica, by contrast, is thick and viscous, trapping gases under enormous pressure until they are released explosively.

Some of the most devastating eruptions in recorded history have come from this second, more explosive category. The eruption of Mount Tambora in Indonesia in 1815 is considered the most powerful volcanic event in recorded human history, ejecting such a vast quantity of ash and sulfur dioxide into the upper atmosphere that global temperatures dropped the following year, leading to widespread crop failures and famine in a period that came to be known as the Year Without a Summer.

Volcanologists monitor active and potentially active volcanoes using a range of techniques, including seismographs to detect the small earthquakes often caused by rising magma, gas sensors to track changes in emissions, and satellite imagery to monitor ground deformation that can indicate magma accumulating beneath the surface. These warning signs do not always allow scientists to predict the exact timing of an eruption, but they can often provide enough advance notice to support evacuations and reduce loss of life.

Despite the destructive power of volcanoes, volcanic activity has also played an essential role in shaping the planet over geological time. Volcanic soils are often exceptionally fertile, supporting dense agricultural populations in regions such as Indonesia, Italy, and parts of Central America. Volcanic activity has also contributed significantly to the formation of the Earth's atmosphere and oceans over billions of years, and continues to create new land, as seen in ongoing eruptions that build new islands and extend existing coastlines.
