Honeybee colonies represent one of the most sophisticated examples of social organization found anywhere in the animal kingdom. A single colony typically contains tens of thousands of individual bees, organized into a strict division of labor built around a single reproductive queen, a relatively small number of male drones, and a large population of female worker bees who carry out nearly all of the colony's day-to-day tasks.

Worker bees progress through a series of roles over the course of their roughly six-week lifespan during the active season. Young workers typically begin by cleaning cells and caring for developing larvae, before moving on to tasks such as building and repairing the wax honeycomb, guarding the hive entrance against intruders, and finally, in the later stages of their lives, foraging outside the hive for nectar and pollen. This age-based progression of roles helps ensure that the colony's various needs are met efficiently as conditions change.

Communication within the colony relies on a remarkable set of behaviors, most famously the waggle dance, first thoroughly described by the biologist Karl von Frisch. Through a series of precise movements, a returning forager can convey both the direction and approximate distance of a valuable food source to other workers in the hive, allowing the colony to rapidly mobilize additional foragers toward productive flower patches, sometimes located kilometers away from the hive itself.

In recent years, honeybee populations in many parts of the world have faced significant pressures, including habitat loss, pesticide exposure, and the spread of parasitic mites and associated viral diseases. The phenomenon known as colony collapse disorder, in which large numbers of worker bees abruptly disappear from otherwise healthy-looking colonies, has drawn particular attention given the vital role honeybees play in pollinating a large share of the world's food crops. Researchers continue to investigate the complex combination of factors likely responsible for these declines, while beekeepers and agricultural organizations have adopted a range of management practices aimed at supporting healthier, more resilient colonies.
