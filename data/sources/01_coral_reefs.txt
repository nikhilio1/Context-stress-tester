Coral reefs are among the most biologically diverse ecosystems on the planet, often compared to rainforests in the sheer density of species they support. They form in shallow, warm, clear ocean waters where sunlight can penetrate easily, since the tiny coral polyps that build reef structures depend on photosynthetic algae living inside their tissues for much of their energy. Over thousands of years, successive generations of coral polyps deposit calcium carbonate skeletons, slowly building the vast limestone structures that make up a reef.

Reefs provide habitat and shelter for an enormous range of marine life, from small fish and crustaceans to larger predators drawn by the abundance of prey. Estimates suggest that although reefs cover less than one percent of the ocean floor, they support roughly a quarter of all known marine species at some stage of their life cycle. Many coastal communities also depend on reefs directly, both for fishing and for the natural barrier they provide against storm surges and coastal erosion.

In recent decades, reef systems around the world have come under serious stress. Rising ocean temperatures linked to climate change have triggered widespread coral bleaching events, in which heat-stressed coral expel the algae living in their tissues, leaving behind a pale, weakened skeleton that is far more vulnerable to disease and death. Ocean acidification, caused by the ocean absorbing increasing amounts of atmospheric carbon dioxide, further threatens reefs by making it harder for coral to build and maintain their calcium carbonate skeletons.

Conservation efforts now range from large-scale marine protected areas to smaller, localized projects such as coral nurseries, where fragments of resilient coral are grown in controlled conditions before being transplanted back onto damaged reefs. Scientists are also studying naturally heat-tolerant coral strains in the hope of breeding or assisting the spread of more resilient populations. Despite these efforts, many researchers warn that without significant reductions in global greenhouse gas emissions, a large proportion of the world's reefs could be lost within the coming decades.
