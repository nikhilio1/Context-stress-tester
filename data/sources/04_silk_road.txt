The Silk Road was not a single road at all, but rather a vast network of overland and maritime trade routes that connected East Asia with the Mediterranean world, stretching across Central Asia, the Indian subcontinent, the Middle East, and parts of East Africa and Europe. The network takes its modern name from the lucrative silk trade that flowed westward out of China, although in reality a huge variety of goods moved along these routes in both directions, including spices, precious stones, metals, textiles, glassware, and paper.

The origins of organized long-distance trade along these routes are often traced back more than two thousand years, to the Han dynasty in China, though trade connections between regions likely existed in more limited forms even earlier. Over the following centuries, the network grew to connect a remarkable number of civilizations, including the Roman and later Byzantine empires, the Persian empires, various Central Asian khanates, and the great trading cities of India and Southeast Asia.

Beyond the exchange of physical goods, the Silk Road served as a conduit for the spread of ideas, religions, technologies, and even diseases. Buddhism spread from India into China and onward into Korea and Japan largely along these trade routes, carried by merchants, monks, and travelers. Papermaking technology, which had been developed in China, gradually made its way westward along the Silk Road, eventually reaching the Islamic world and later Europe, where it would help enable the later spread of printed books.

The network's importance gradually declined with the rise of maritime trade routes in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, as European powers developed ships capable of sailing directly to Asia around the southern tip of Africa, bypassing the overland routes entirely. Even so, the historical and cultural legacy of the Silk Road remains significant, and the term is still used today to describe modern infrastructure and trade initiatives aimed at reconnecting Asia, Europe, and Africa through new transportation and economic corridors.
