Long before the invention of the printing press, several great libraries of the ancient world served as centers of learning, collecting and preserving written knowledge from across their known world. Perhaps the most famous of these was the Library of Alexandria, founded in Egypt in the early third century BCE under the patronage of the Ptolemaic rulers who succeeded Alexander the Great. At its height, the library is believed to have housed hundreds of thousands of papyrus scrolls, covering subjects ranging from mathematics and astronomy to medicine, philosophy, and literature.

Alexandria attracted scholars from across the Mediterranean world and beyond, drawn by access to its vast collection and by royal patronage that supported their research. Many significant advances in mathematics, geography, and astronomy are associated with scholars who worked at or were connected to the library, including early calculations of the circumference of the Earth and foundational work in geometry that would influence mathematical thought for centuries afterward.

The eventual decline and loss of the Library of Alexandria remains a subject of historical debate, with evidence suggesting the collection was diminished gradually over several centuries through a combination of factors, including political instability, reduced funding, and possibly a series of fires, rather than being destroyed in a single catastrophic event as is sometimes popularly portrayed. Regardless of the precise circumstances, the loss of so much accumulated knowledge is often cited as one of history's great cultural tragedies.

Other notable libraries of the ancient and medieval world included the Library of Pergamon in Asia Minor, which reportedly rivaled Alexandria's collection, and later, the House of Wisdom in Baghdad, which became a major center of translation and scholarship during the Islamic Golden Age, preserving and building upon Greek, Persian, and Indian texts. These institutions, though separated by centuries and geography, collectively illustrate a long-standing human impulse to gather, organize, and protect written knowledge, an impulse that would eventually find new expression through the printing press and, much later, digital archives and the internet.
